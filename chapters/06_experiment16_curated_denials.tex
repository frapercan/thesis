\subsection{Experiment~16: What a Curated Denial Was Telling Us}
\label{sec:exp16-curated-denials}

A negative annotation is not a gap in the record. When a curator writes that a protein
does \emph{not} have a function, that statement carries evidence, a reference and a
date, exactly as a positive one does. It is also, almost always, a statement somebody
took the trouble to make because there was a reason to believe the opposite, and in
this field the reason is nearly always a homologue.

That makes the negative annotation the one class of record in the corpus aimed squarely
at the error a homology-transfer method makes. It marks the places where similarity is
known to mislead. A method that transfers annotation between neighbours and ignores
those marks is not merely uninformed: it is walking into traps whose locations have
been published.

This experiment asks how often that happens, and the answer is half the time.

\subsubsection{Design, and Why It Cannot Leak}

The denials are read from the reference bank alone, the annotation corpus as it stood
at the start of the evaluation window. Nothing is read from the truth set. This matters
because a denial recorded \emph{after} the cutoff would be a statement about the answer,
and filtering with it would be reading the answer under another name. The two corpora
differ: the bank holds 5{,}603 negative annotations and the later truth set holds
5{,}397. Only the first is consulted, at prediction time and at every later step.

The predictions examined are one arm of the backbone sweep at retrieval depth thirty,
2{,}441{,}584 candidate rows over 14{,}032 query proteins, scored under the deployed
scoring configuration. Of the 5{,}603 denials in the bank, 936 distinct (protein, term) denials concern a
protein in the query set. Those 936 are the population of this experiment: statements
the method could have consulted and did not. Counting each evidence code separately
gives 956, and the aspect breakdown below is reported that way because the evidence is
part of what makes a denial informative; every count in the propagation analysis uses
the distinct 936.

The aggregate is half, and the breakdown is worse than the aggregate for the aspects
that matter most to this method. Molecular function and cellular component are where
homology transfer is most confident, because catalytic activity and subcellular
localisation are the properties that most reliably survive between close relatives.
They are also, here, the two aspects where the method most often asserts precisely
what a curator has denied. Biological process, the aspect where transfer is weakest
and where the system's own measured performance is lowest, is the aspect where it
violates fewest denials, because it proposes those terms less confidently in the first
place.

The ordering is therefore the opposite of reassuring. The error is not concentrated
where the method is weak. It is concentrated where the method is strong.

\subsubsection{The Violations Are Not at the Tail}

A defect that lived among marginal candidates would be an argument for a stricter cut.
This one does not.

Predicted in first place & 115 \\

Sixty per cent of the violations sit inside the query's own top five, and a quarter of
them are the single best candidate the method produced for that protein. The donors
carrying them are close: a median cosine distance of 0.0129 places them among the
nearest neighbours in the reference bank, not at the edge of the retrieved
neighbourhood. No threshold on distance and no reduction of retrieval depth reaches
this error, because the error is made with the method's most confident evidence.

\subsubsection{One Protein, in Full}

The clearest case is \texttt{O94526}, the PTEN homologue of \emph{Schizosaccharomyces
pombe}. The bank records:

\begin{quote}
\texttt{NOT|enables GO:0004439 phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate\\ 5-phosphatase activity (IDA)}
\end{quote}

The method predicted that exact term, in first place, transferred from \texttt{P40559}
at a cosine distance of 0.0151.

The whole problem is in that row. PTEN homologues are lipid phosphatases; that is what
the family is known for, and it is why a nearest neighbour carrying the activity is a
persuasive donor. A curator assayed this particular protein directly, found that it
does not have the activity, and published the negative result. The annotation exists
\emph{because} the homology is misleading in this instance. The method used the
homology, did not consult the annotation, and produced the error the annotation had
been written to prevent.

A second case has the same shape in a different aspect. \texttt{Q9Y468} carries
\texttt{NOT|located\_in GO:0005730 nucleolus} by direct assay, and the method predicted
nucleolar localisation at rank two, from \texttt{Q96JM7} at a distance of 0.0090.

\subsubsection{A Denial Constrains a Subtree, and What That Is Worth}

A positive annotation propagates upward: a protein annotated with a child term is
thereby annotated with the parent. The corresponding statement for a negative
annotation is its contrapositive, and it propagates in the opposite direction. If a
protein does not have the parent, it cannot have any descendant of the parent, since
having the descendant would entail having the parent.

The logic is sound, but its practical value is smaller than it first appears, and the
reason is worth stating because it is easy to overcount. The evaluator closes
predictions over the ontology before scoring. Once it has, checking the \emph{named}
denied term against the closed prediction set is exactly equivalent to checking the
downward-propagated denial against the raw one: both fire when the method predicted the
denied term or any of its descendants. Downward propagation is therefore not an
additional source of violations. It is the same test, performed at the other end.

What it does buy is the violations that a check against raw, unclosed predictions would
miss:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Denials violated, counted per denial rather than per predicted term.}
\label{tab:exp16-propagation}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & Denials & Rate \\
\midrule
Distinct denials on query proteins & 936 & \\
Violated at the named term, raw predictions & 465 & 49.7\% \\
Violated once predictions are ancestor-closed & 527 & 56.3\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The increase is 62 denials, or 13 per cent, and not the multiple that a careless count
would report. Those 62 are not a hypothetical: they are the specification the deployed
filter meets. It seeds on the direct denials and descends the graph, so it tests the
subtree rather than the named term, and the 527 is therefore a contract the code
satisfies by construction rather than a bound on what happens.

The descent runs along \texttt{is\_a} and \texttt{part\_of} and no further, which is
correct and not obvious. Those are the relations an annotation is defined to travel, so
they are the ones along which the entailment that makes a denial descend actually
holds. A regulation edge is not subsumption: a protein that does not have a process is
not thereby barred from regulating it, and propagating a denial along that edge would
assert something the ontology does not. The careless count is worth naming, because it is the mistake this
measurement invites: 1{,}185 candidate rows fall inside a denied subtree, and comparing
that against 465 suggests a factor of two and a half. It is not one. The first number
counts predicted \emph{pairs} and the second counts violated \emph{denials}, and a
single denial violated by four predicted descendants contributes four to the one and one
to the other. The pair count is the right quantity for sizing what a filter removes and
the wrong one for sizing how often the method errs.

The two framings also disagree about the density of the defect, and both are true:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{The same defect under two denominators.}
\label{tab:exp16-density}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Question & Numerator & Denominator \\
\midrule
How often is a named denial violated & 465 & 936 \quad (49.7\%) \\
How often is a denied pair predicted & 1{,}185 & 39{,}105 \quad (3.0\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Half of the named denials are violated, and three per cent of the pairs a denial
excludes are predicted. The first answers how often the method contradicts a curator
where a curator has spoken. The second answers how much of a guard a denial is once it
is extended to everything it logically forbids, and the answer is that most of what a
denial forbids was never at risk of being predicted. Reporting only the first overstates
how much work the subtree does; reporting only the second understates how often the
method is wrong where it has been warned.

\subsubsection{The Remedy, and Three Choices It Records}

The filter removes candidates that the bank denies about the query, and it is
implemented in the evaluation frame so that it applies to every arm without any
retrieval being repeated. Three design decisions in it are worth recording, because
each one addresses a defect this system has exhibited more than once.

\paragraph{The policy reads its own inputs.} The reference bank and the ontology are
resolved from the prediction set's own row rather than passed in by the caller. This is
not a convenience. A depth restriction added earlier to this same pipeline spent an
entire campaign silently unapplied because it was a field that two construction sites
each had to remember to populate, and neither did. A check whose inputs come from the
record it is about cannot be forgotten into being inactive.

\paragraph{Every writer applies it.} There are three code paths that write predictions,
and the one the campaign actually uses never constructs the cached candidate frame the
other two share. A filter applied by some writers and not others is the same defect in
different clothing, and it would have been invisible in exactly the configuration that
matters.

\paragraph{The ontology is the prediction set's own.} Propagation runs on the snapshot
the predictions were made under, not on the snapshot the evaluation pivots to. The two
disagree by 29 pairs. Using the later one would import ontology structure created after
the cutoff, which is a small leak but a leak of the same kind as any other.

\subsubsection{What This Settles and What It Does Not}

The 1{,}190 removed pairs are a small fraction of 2{,}441{,}584, and the change does not
move a headline measure. The filter was not built for the measure.

What the experiment establishes is diagnostic, and it is three things. The method is
confidently wrong precisely where the corpus has taken the trouble to say so, on half
of the denials that concern its queries. It is wrong there at rank one, which no
threshold on distance or depth can reach. And the subtree a denial forbids adds 13 per
cent more violated denials than its named term alone, which is a real increase and a
much smaller one than the pair count invites.

Three things are left open, and the first is the one that matters for the method rather
than for its bookkeeping.

The 5{,}603 negative annotations constitute a labelled negative set, and nothing in this
system learns from them. Filtering removes a wrong answer after it has been proposed. A
method trained against these records would not propose it, and the difference between
those two is the difference between a guard and a model.

Nothing here tests whether a query carries an \emph{implicit} denial, meaning a term
inconsistent with what the protein is already known to be rather than one a curator
explicitly ruled out. That set is far larger than 5{,}603 and defining it requires the
ontology's own structure rather than its annotations.

And the threefold difference in violation rate across aspects has no explanation. If
cellular component stands at 68 per cent because localisation transfers badly between
homologues in ways that function does not, that is a statement about the method reaching
well beyond these records, and it is measurable on the same corpus.
