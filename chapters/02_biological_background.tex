\chapter{Biological Background}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter provides the biological and computational foundation required to
understand the protein function annotation problem that PROTEA addresses.
The presentation moves from first principles (proteins, sequences, structure)
through the formalised vocabulary for function (Gene Ontology), the practical
bottleneck of curation, classical alignment-based approaches and their limits
in the twilight zone, and finally to protein language models as the
embedding substrate that makes large-scale automated annotation feasible.
Readers with a strong background in structural bioinformatics may skip to
\cref{sec:gene-ontology}; readers already familiar with the GO may proceed
to \cref{sec:plm}.

\section{Proteins: Sequence, Structure, and Function}
\label{sec:proteins}

Proteins are the principal molecular effectors of biology. Every cellular
process --- catalysis, signal transduction, mechanical support, molecular
transport, immune defence --- is carried out by proteins acting alone or in
complexes. Understanding \textit{what a protein does} is therefore
fundamental to every branch of biology and medicine.

\subsection{Chemical Composition}
\label{subsec:amino-acids}

A protein is a linear polymer of \textit{amino acid} residues joined by
peptide bonds. The twenty standard amino acids share a common backbone
(an $\alpha$-carbon flanked by an amino group and a carboxyl group) but
differ in their side-chain chemistry, which ranges from non-polar aliphatic
(alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine) through aromatic (phenylalanine,
tyrosine, tryptophan) to polar (serine, threonine, asparagine, glutamine),
positively charged (lysine, arginine, histidine), negatively charged
(aspartate, glutamate), and chemically special (cysteine forms disulphide
bonds; proline introduces a rigid kink; glycine has no side chain and
confers flexibility).

This 20-letter alphabet defines the \textit{primary structure} of a protein:
its amino acid sequence. A protein of $n$ residues can in principle
occupy $20^n$ distinct sequences, an astronomically large combinatorial space.
The sequences that fold into stable, functional structures occupy a tiny and
geometrically structured subset of this space, one in which local sequence
patterns constrain the global fold and the functional residues are often
spatially concentrated in active sites or binding pockets.

\subsection{From Sequence to Structure}
\label{subsec:folding}

The primary sequence encodes all the information necessary for a protein to
adopt its native three-dimensional conformation, a result established by
Anfinsen's renaturation experiments on ribonuclease~A in the early 1960s
\cite{anfinsen1973principles}. This \textit{thermodynamic hypothesis} states
that the native fold corresponds to the global free-energy minimum of the
polypeptide in its physiological environment, so that the sequence uniquely
determines the structure under given conditions.

The spatial organisation of a protein is described at four levels:

\begin{description}
  \item[Primary structure] The amino acid sequence itself.
  \item[Secondary structure] Local regularities arising from backbone
    hydrogen bonds: $\alpha$-helices, in which a residue donates a hydrogen
    bond to the residue four positions ahead, and $\beta$-strands, which
    associate laterally into sheets. Loops and turns connect these elements.
  \item[Tertiary structure] The overall three-dimensional fold of a single
    polypeptide chain, determined by packing of secondary structure elements,
    hydrophobic collapse of the core, and stabilising interactions (disulphide
    bonds, salt bridges, van der Waals contacts).
  \item[Quaternary structure] The assembly of multiple polypeptide chains
    (subunits) into a functional complex; haemoglobin's four-subunit $\alpha_2
    \beta_2$ tetramer is a classic example.
\end{description}

The sequence-structure-function paradigm holds that the sequence determines the
structure (Anfinsen), and the structure determines the function. This
chain of causality is the conceptual anchor for all
computation-based approaches to function prediction: if two proteins share
similar sequences, they likely share similar folds, which in turn suggests
similar functions.

\subsection{The AlphaFold Revolution}
\label{subsec:alphafold}

For decades, determining a protein structure experimentally (by X-ray
crystallography, NMR, or cryo-electron microscopy) was the rate-limiting step
in structural biology. In 2021, DeepMind's AlphaFold~2
\cite{jumper2021alphafold} achieved near-experimental accuracy on the
Critical Assessment of Protein Structure Prediction (CASP14) benchmark,
effectively solving the protein structure prediction problem for most
single-domain proteins. The subsequent AlphaFold Protein Structure Database
(AFDB) \cite{varadi2022afdb} released predicted structures for over 200
million proteins from UniProt, providing structural information at a scale
that experimental determination cannot approach.

AlphaFold~2 uses multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) as evolutionary context,
feeding coevolution signals into a novel attention mechanism (the Evoformer)
before iterative structural refinement. The key insight is that correlated
mutations across homologous sequences encode contact information, and a deep
network can decode these correlations into accurate 3D coordinates.

For PROTEA the AlphaFold era is relevant for two reasons. First, the
availability of predicted structures has expanded structure-informed function
prediction beyond the set of experimentally characterised proteins. Second,
and more immediately relevant, protein language models trained on sequence
corpora have been shown to implicitly recapitulate structural signals
(\cref{sec:plm}), making structure-derived embeddings largely substitutable
by sequence-only PLM embeddings for the downstream annotation transfer task.

\section{Protein Databases}
\label{sec:databases}

\subsection{UniProt}
\label{subsec:uniprot}

\gls{uniprot} is the central repository for protein sequence and functional
information \cite{uniprot2023}. It is divided into two sections:

\begin{description}
  \item[\gls{swissprot}] A manually curated, high-quality database. Each
    entry has been reviewed by expert annotators and contains experimental
    or computationally inferred functional annotations with explicit evidence
    codes. As of 2024, Swiss-Prot contains approximately 570,000 entries.
  \item[\gls{trembl}] An automatically annotated, computationally analysed
    database derived from genome translations. TrEMBL is several orders of
    magnitude larger (over 250 million sequences as of 2024) but of lower
    average annotation quality; most entries carry only
    \texttt{IEA} (Inferred from Electronic Annotation) evidence codes.
\end{description}

Each protein in UniProt is identified by a stable accession number. Canonical
isoforms are represented by a bare accession (e.g.\ \texttt{P12345});
alternative splicing variants are denoted \texttt{P12345-2},
\texttt{P12345-3}, and so on.

UniProt-GOA is the annotation dataset derived from UniProt, providing
the mapping between UniProt accessions and Gene Ontology terms. It is the
principal source of training labels for supervised function prediction
systems, including PROTEA. The scale asymmetry between Swiss-Prot and
TrEMBL is fundamental to the annotation bottleneck discussed in
\cref{sec:annotation-bottleneck}.

\subsection{Protein Data Bank}
\label{subsec:pdb}

The \gls{pdb} archives three-dimensional structures of biological
macromolecules determined experimentally by X-ray crystallography,
cryo-electron microscopy, or NMR \cite{berman2000pdb}. Although PROTEA
operates primarily on sequences, PDB structures are an important source of
ground-truth functional information and are used indirectly through UniProt
cross-references. Structural homologues in the PDB can carry experimental
annotations that are transferred to uncharacterised queries via either
alignment-based or embedding-based methods.

\section{The Gene Ontology}
\label{sec:gene-ontology}

\subsection{Motivation and Overview}
\label{subsec:go-overview}

Before the Gene Ontology existed, functional annotations were scattered across
organism-specific databases, each with its own vocabulary. A catalytic
activity called ``kinase'' in one database might appear as
``phosphotransferase'' in another; cross-species comparison required manual
reconciliation. The Gene Ontology Consortium was formed in 1998 to solve
this problem by providing a single, species-independent, formally structured
vocabulary for describing gene product attributes
\cite{ashburner2000go,goconsortium2023}.

The GO is structured as three independent \glspl{dag}, one for each of the
three top-level \textit{aspects}:

\begin{description}
  \item[\gls{mf} (GO:0003674)] The biochemical activity of the gene product,
    independent of where or when it occurs (e.g.\ \textit{ATP binding},
    \textit{serine-type endopeptidase activity}). MFO terms describe
    \textit{what} the protein does at the molecular level.
  \item[\gls{bp} (GO:0008150)] The larger biological programme to which the
    molecular activity contributes (e.g.\ \textit{apoptotic process},
    \textit{DNA repair}, \textit{carbohydrate metabolic process}). BPO terms
    are typically more specific to cellular context than MFO terms and often
    have hundreds of ancestors up to the root.
  \item[\gls{cc} (GO:0005575)] The location in the cell where the gene
    product is active or where it can be found (e.g.\ \textit{nucleus},
    \textit{plasma membrane}, \textit{extracellular matrix}). CCO terms form
    the shallowest hierarchy of the three aspects.
\end{description}

The three aspects share no terms and have separate root nodes; a gene product
is annotated independently in each aspect in which it has known function.
As of the 2024 release, the ontology contains approximately 43,000 terms
across the three aspects, with BPO being the largest
($\approx$29,000 terms), MFO intermediate ($\approx$11,000), and CCO the
smallest ($\approx$4,000).

\subsection{DAG Structure and the True Path Rule}
\label{subsec:go-dag}

Within each aspect, GO terms are connected by typed relations. The two most
common are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{is-a}: a term is a more specific case of its parent. For
    example, \textit{serine-type endopeptidase activity} is-a
    \textit{endopeptidase activity} is-a \textit{peptidase activity} is-a
    \textit{hydrolase activity} is-a \textit{catalytic activity}.
  \item \textit{part-of}: a term represents a component or subprocess of
    its parent. For example, \textit{G1/S transition of mitotic cell cycle}
    part-of \textit{mitotic cell cycle}.
\end{itemize}

Additional relations (\textit{regulates}, \textit{positively regulates},
\textit{negatively regulates}, \textit{has part}) appear in more recent
versions of the ontology, primarily in the BPO aspect.

The fundamental consequence of this DAG structure is the
\textit{true path rule}: if a gene product is annotated with a GO term $t$,
it is implicitly annotated with all ancestors of $t$ up to the root of the
corresponding aspect. This propagation rule is universally applied when
constructing annotation datasets for function prediction experiments and
when evaluating predicted annotations against ground truth
\cite{clark2013information}.

The true path rule has important implications for evaluation. Because
predicting a specific leaf term is strictly harder than predicting its
ancestor, methods that predict at the leaf level can be rewarded or
penalised differently depending on how the evaluation metric treats
the ancestor-descendant hierarchy. The Fmax metric used in CAFA
\cite{radivojac2013cafa1} aggregates precision and recall over all propagated
annotations, handling this correctly.

\subsection{Evidence Codes}
\label{subsec:evidence-codes}

Every GO annotation is tagged with an evidence code that indicates the type
of support for the association. Evidence codes fall into three broad classes:

\paragraph{Experimental evidence codes.} These are the gold standard,
signifying that the annotation was supported by direct experimental
observation of the gene product:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{EXP}: Inferred from Experiment (general).
  \item \texttt{IDA}: Inferred from Direct Assay.
  \item \texttt{IPI}: Inferred from Physical Interaction.
  \item \texttt{IMP}: Inferred from Mutant Phenotype.
  \item \texttt{IGI}: Inferred from Genetic Interaction.
  \item \texttt{IEP}: Inferred from Expression Pattern.
  \item \texttt{HTP}: Inferred from High Throughput Experiment.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Computational analysis codes.} These codes indicate that the
annotation was derived computationally rather than from direct experiment:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{ISS}: Inferred from Sequence or Structural Similarity.
  \item \texttt{ISO}: Inferred from Sequence Orthology.
  \item \texttt{ISA}: Inferred from Sequence Alignment.
  \item \texttt{ISM}: Inferred from Sequence Model.
  \item \texttt{IGC}: Inferred from Genomic Context.
  \item \texttt{IBA}: Inferred from Biological aspect of Ancestor
    (PANTHER-based phylogenetic annotations).
  \item \texttt{IEA}: Inferred from Electronic Annotation (see below).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Author-stated codes.} \texttt{TAS} (Traceable Author Statement)
and \texttt{NAS} (Non-traceable Author Statement) indicate annotations
extracted from published papers without experimental evidence in the strict
sense.

\paragraph{The IEA class in depth.}
\texttt{IEA} (Inferred from Electronic Annotation) deserves particular
attention because it accounts for the large majority of GO annotations in
UniProt-GOA. IEA annotations are generated by automated pipelines that
transfer annotations from characterised homologues without human review. The
principal sources of IEA annotations include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{UniProt rule-based annotation:} HAMAP, RuleBase, and
    SAAS systems apply expert-curated rules based on sequence family
    membership.
  \item \textbf{InterPro/Pfam mapping:} Domain matches to Pfam
    \cite{mistry2021pfam} and other InterPro \cite{blum2021interpro} member
    databases are associated with GO terms via manually curated cross-references.
  \item \textbf{Ensembl Compara orthology:} GO terms are propagated from
    experimentally characterised orthologues in model organisms.
\end{itemize}

Because IEA annotations are not subject to manual review, they are routinely
excluded from CAFA benchmarks, which restrict ground truth to experimental
evidence codes. The consequence is a sharp contrast in database coverage:
while TrEMBL contains over 250 million sequences, only a few million of these
carry experimental annotations, and the rest are annotated either by IEA
or not at all.

\subsection{Ontology Versioning}
\label{subsec:go-versioning}

The GO is actively maintained and released regularly in \gls{obo} and OWL
format. New terms are added as biological knowledge expands, obsolete terms
are deprecated and replaced, and relationship types are refined. Reproducible
research requires recording which ontology version was used. PROTEA stores
each imported release as an \texttt{OntologySnapshot} entity and associates
all annotation sets and predictions with a specific snapshot, ensuring that
evaluation metrics are always computed against the same DAG.

\section{The Annotation Bottleneck}
\label{sec:annotation-bottleneck}

The gap between the number of known protein sequences and the number of
experimentally characterised proteins is enormous and widening. High-throughput
sequencing has made it trivial to identify millions of new protein sequences
from environmental samples, single-cell transcriptomics, and metagenomics; the
cost of adding a sequence to a database is effectively zero. The cost of
determining its function experimentally, by contrast, involves labour-intensive
biochemical assays, targeted genetic experiments, or X-ray crystallography,
and scales linearly with the number of proteins to be characterised.

As of 2024, UniProtKB contains over 250 million sequences, but Swiss-Prot
covers only approximately 570,000 manually reviewed entries. Even within
Swiss-Prot, the fraction of entries with at least one experimental GO
annotation in a given aspect is far below 100\,\%: many entries carry only
ISS or IEA codes. For TrEMBL, the situation is starker: the overwhelming
majority of entries have no experimentally verified functional annotation.

The bottleneck has direct consequences for biology and medicine:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Metagenomics:} Large-scale environmental sequencing
    projects (the Ocean Microbiome Project, the Earth Microbiome Project)
    generate millions of novel protein families, most of which remain
    functionally unknown.
  \item \textbf{Drug discovery:} Novel disease targets require functional
    characterisation before they can be therapeutically exploited. Unannotated
    proteins from pathogen proteomes or disease-specific expression profiles
    cannot be prioritised without some functional signal.
  \item \textbf{Evolutionary biology:} Understanding the functional
    diversification of protein families requires dense annotation across
    taxonomic lineages, which experimental methods cannot provide at scale.
\end{itemize}

Computational function prediction exists to alleviate this bottleneck. The
CAFA challenges \cite{radivojac2013cafa1,jiang2016cafa2,zhou2019cafa3}
provide a community benchmarking framework that measures how well automated
methods predict the experimental annotations that were withheld from
participating teams. PROTEA's design and evaluation are framed within this
CAFA context.

\section{Sequence Alignment and Its Limits}
\label{sec:alignment}

The classical approach to inferring function from sequence rests on the
principle that similar sequences imply shared ancestry (homology), and
homologous proteins typically perform the same function. Sequence alignment
algorithms formalise sequence similarity as an optimisation problem.

\subsection{Pairwise Alignment Algorithms}
\label{subsec:pairwise-alignment}

Two algorithms form the foundation of pairwise alignment:

\begin{description}
  \item[Needleman-Wunsch (\gls{nw})] A global alignment algorithm that aligns
    two sequences end-to-end, maximising a cumulative substitution score
    minus gap penalties \cite{needleman1970general}. Needleman-Wunsch is
    appropriate for proteins of similar length where the alignment is expected
    to span the full sequence.
  \item[Smith-Waterman (\gls{sw})] A local alignment algorithm that finds the
    highest-scoring contiguous aligned region without penalising the
    unaligned tails \cite{smith1981identification}. Smith-Waterman is more
    appropriate when one sequence is a domain or fragment of the other, or
    when only one functional domain is conserved.
\end{description}

Both algorithms use a \textit{substitution matrix} to score residue pairs in
the alignment. BLOSUM62 \cite{henikoff1992amino} is the most widely used
matrix for protein sequences; it is derived from the observed substitution
frequencies in conserved blocks (ungapped alignments) across protein families
with $\le 62$\,\% pairwise identity, capturing the empirical exchangeability
of amino acids in an evolutionary context.

\subsection{Database Search and Iterative Profile Methods}
\label{subsec:blast-hmmer}

Direct pairwise alignment of a query against a database of millions of
sequences is computationally prohibitive. BLAST \cite{altschul1990blast}
solves this by a heuristic: it first finds short exact matches (seeds) and
then extends these into high-scoring segment pairs (HSPs), discarding
sequence pairs with no seed before investing in full alignment. The trade-off
is a small loss in sensitivity relative to Smith-Waterman.

For more distant homologues, iterative profile methods significantly
outperform single-sequence queries. PSI-BLAST \cite{altschul1997psiblast}
constructs a position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) from the hits of a
first BLAST round and uses it in subsequent rounds, progressively detecting
more divergent relatives. Profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) as implemented
in HMMER \cite{eddy2011hmmer} generalise this idea to a full probabilistic
framework: multiple sequence alignments of known protein families are
compiled into HMM profiles, and a query is scored against all profiles in a
database. Profile-vs-profile comparison (HHpred) achieves even greater
sensitivity. Park et al.\ \cite{park1998sequence} showed that multiple-sequence
profiles detect roughly three times as many remote homologues as pairwise
methods.

\subsection{The Twilight Zone of Sequence Similarity}
\label{subsec:twilight}

Despite their power, alignment-based methods break down at low sequence
identity. Rost's seminal analysis \cite{rost1999twilight} established that
below approximately $25$--$30$\,\% pairwise identity the relationship between
sequence similarity and structural (and functional) similarity becomes
statistically unreliable: proteins in this range may be structurally similar
yet functionally diverged (paralogues recruited to new roles), or functionally
similar yet structurally distinct (convergent evolution). This region is
known as the \textit{twilight zone} of sequence alignments.

The twilight zone is not merely a theoretical concern. A substantial fraction
of the proteins targeted by CAFA challenges have best-hit alignment identities
below $30$\,\% to any Swiss-Prot entry carrying an experimental annotation.
For these proteins, alignment-based annotation transfer is unreliable, and
methods that capture higher-order evolutionary and functional signal are
necessary.

\paragraph{Beyond the twilight zone.}
At identity below $\approx 20$\,\%, alignment-based methods provide essentially
no signal; this region is sometimes called the \textit{midnight zone}. In
practice, tools such as HHpred can detect structural homologues even at
near-zero sequence identity by comparing profile-to-profile, but at the cost
of substantial computational effort and with a much higher false-positive rate.

The twilight and midnight zones define the target domain for PROTEA's
embedding-based approach: if an alignment-based tool cannot reliably transfer
annotations, but a protein language model embedding places the query close to
functionally similar proteins in a high-dimensional latent space, then
embedding-based KNN annotation transfer can recover signal that classical
methods miss.

\section{Protein Language Models and Embeddings}
\label{sec:plm}

\subsection{The Language Model Analogy}
\label{subsec:plm-analogy}

Protein sequences can be treated as sentences over a 20-letter alphabet,
in direct analogy to natural language sentences over a word vocabulary.
This analogy is not merely superficial: both natural language and protein
sequences exhibit long-range statistical dependencies (a residue at one end of
a protein can be constrained by residues hundreds of positions away through
structural contacts), hierarchical composition (secondary structure elements
compose into domains, domains compose into multi-domain proteins), and
generative diversity (a small fraction of the space of possible sequences is
grammatically or biochemically valid).

Natural language processing models trained with self-supervised objectives
on large text corpora, particularly the Transformer architecture
\cite{vaswani2017attention} with masked language modelling (MLM) or autoregressive
generation, learn rich contextual representations of words. The same
approach applied to protein sequences yields \glspl{plm}: neural networks
that learn to predict masked or future residues from context, developing
internal representations that capture evolutionary, structural, and
functional signal without explicit supervision on any of these targets.

The critical point is that PLM representations are \textit{emergent}: the
model is trained only to reconstruct sequences, yet the representations
it learns encode information about structure and function because this
information is implicit in the statistical patterns of sequence evolution.
Proteins that perform similar functions tend to occupy similar regions of
the PLM embedding space, even when their sequences have diverged beyond the
twilight zone of alignment-based detection.

\subsection{The 2020--2024 Generation: Sequence-Only Encoders}
\label{subsec:plm-seq}

The first generation of practical \glspl{plm} for function prediction was
based on Transformer encoders trained with masked language modelling on
sequence-only corpora.

\textbf{ESM-1b} \cite{rives2021esm1b} (Meta AI) is a 650M-parameter model
trained on UniRef50 with a MLM objective on 250 million protein sequences.
Rives et al.\ demonstrated that the representations spontaneously encode
structural features (contact maps derivable from attention patterns) and
support downstream function prediction with linear probes. ESM-1b was
a landmark result showing that biological structure and function emerge
from unsupervised learning on sequences alone.

\textbf{ESM-2} \cite{lin2023esm2} expanded this framework to eight model
sizes from 8M to 15B parameters, trained on UniRef90. Each scale increment
yields measurable gains on structure prediction (ESMFold uses ESM-2 as its
trunk) and remote-homology detection. PROTEA evaluates ESM-2 at three
operating points (650M, 3B, 15B) to characterise the accuracy/throughput
trade-off relevant to a production annotation system.

\textbf{ProtTrans} \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans} provides a suite of encoder
models (ProtBERT, ProtAlbert, ProtXLNet, ProtT5) trained on \gls{bfd} and
UniRef100 using distributed HPC resources. ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 achieves
the best per-residue and per-protein accuracy among the ProtTrans variants
and remains a widely used baseline.

\textbf{Ankh} \cite{elnaggar2023ankh} is an encoder-decoder model trained
on UniRef50 with a focused objective and aggressive vocabulary tuning.
It reports state-of-the-art accuracy on several downstream tasks at a
fraction of the parameter count of the largest ESM-2 variants, making it
an attractive operating point when GPU memory is constrained.

\subsection{The 2024--2025 Generation: Structure-Aware and Distilled Models}
\label{subsec:plm-modern}

Three trends shape the most recent generation of PLMs: integration of
structural signal during training, further parameter efficiency through
distillation, and multi-modal sequence-plus-structure representations.

\textbf{ProstT5} \cite{heinzinger2023prostt5} extends ProtT5 with a bilingual
sequence-to-3Di translation objective. The 3Di alphabet is a structural
discretisation developed for Foldseek \cite{vankempen2024foldseek}, in which
each residue is assigned one of 20 structural states based on its local
geometric context. By training on both sequence and 3Di tokens simultaneously,
ProstT5 produces embeddings that encode structural information without
requiring an explicit 3D input; the model learns the mapping from sequence
statistics to structural states, effectively internalising structure-awareness.

\textbf{ESM-3} \cite{hayes2024esm3} is a multi-modal generative model trained
over sequence, structure, and function tokens jointly, reaching parameter
scales up to 98B. For embedding-based downstream applications PROTEA uses
the lighter component encoder (ESM-3c), which provides per-residue and
per-protein embeddings with an interface compatible with the ESM-2 family.

\textbf{ESM-C} \cite{esm2024esmc} (released in late 2024 by EvolutionaryScale)
is a family of efficient sequence-only encoders distilled from larger ESM-3
variants. ESM-C 300M and 600M deliver embeddings that are competitive with
ESM-2 3B at a fraction of the inference cost, making them the smallest
backends at which PROTEA achieves strong CAFA-style performance.

\subsection{Embeddings as Biology-Aware Representations}
\label{subsec:plm-biology}

Littmann et al.\ \cite{littmann2021embeddings} demonstrated empirically that
ProtT5 embeddings transfer GO annotations beyond the homology detection
threshold: for proteins with no detectable alignment to any annotated
sequence, nearest-neighbour transfer in embedding space still achieves
meaningful precision. This result grounds the core hypothesis of PROTEA's
annotation engine: embedding proximity is a proxy for functional similarity
even in the twilight zone, because evolutionary pressure preserves function
more conservatively than sequence.

The biological justification operates through the \textit{homology continuum}.
Natural proteins do not fall cleanly into ``homologous'' and
``non-homologous'' categories; rather, there is a continuous spectrum of
evolutionary relatedness, and PLM embeddings reflect this continuum. Two
proteins may share only $15$\,\% sequence identity yet be clearly homologous
by structural and functional criteria; their embeddings will be closer in
the PLM space than a random pair at the same identity level, because the
model has learned to weight evolutionarily constrained positions more heavily.

This line of reasoning justifies PLM-KNN as a biology-coherent approach:
the K nearest neighbours in embedding space are, on average, more likely
to be functionally related than the K most sequence-similar proteins retrieved
by BLAST, particularly in the twilight and midnight zones.

\subsection{Inference Cost and the Operating-Point Question}
\label{subsec:plm-cost}

Each PLM occupies a distinct point on the accuracy/throughput trade-off
curve. ESM-2 15B produces the strongest embeddings but requires several
days on a single A100 GPU to embed 500,000 sequences; ESM-C 300M delivers
embeddings of comparable downstream quality on the same hardware in under
a day. Choosing a backend is therefore a deployment decision as much as
a modelling one, and a platform that operationalises PLM-based annotation
at scale must abstract this choice cleanly. \Cref{chap:design} describes
how PROTEA implements that abstraction through the \texttt{EmbeddingBackend}
plugin contract, allowing the backend to be swapped without modifying the
annotation pipeline.

This chapter does not compare PLM architectures in depth; that comparison,
together with an analysis of competing methods (DeepGO, NetGO, PANNZER2,
etc.), is deferred to \cref{chap:related}.

\section{Taxonomic Classification}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Biological organisms are classified into a hierarchy (the NCBI taxonomy)
that reflects their evolutionary relationships \cite{schoch2020ncbi}.
Each node is assigned a numeric \textit{taxon ID}. The distance between two
organisms can be defined operationally as the number of edges between them
through their \gls{lca} in the taxonomy tree.

Taxonomic distance is a useful covariate for function prediction: a protein
from a closely related organism is more likely to perform the same function
as the query than one from a distant lineage, even when embedding similarity
is comparable. PROTEA incorporates taxonomy as a soft scoring factor in its
KNN ranking, downweighting neighbours from taxonomically distant species for
query proteins whose taxon is well represented in the reference database.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:background-summary}

This chapter has laid the conceptual foundation for the rest of the thesis:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Proteins are polymers whose sequence (primary structure) determines
    their three-dimensional fold (Anfinsen's thermodynamic hypothesis), which
    in turn determines their biological function.
  \item The Gene Ontology provides a species-independent formal vocabulary
    for protein function, organised as three DAG aspects (BPO, MFO, CCO)
    with evidence codes that distinguish experimental from computational
    annotations.
  \item The annotation bottleneck arises because the number of known
    sequences far exceeds the number experimentally characterised; this gap
    motivates computational function prediction at scale.
  \item Classical sequence alignment works well when pairwise identity is
    above approximately $30$\,\%, but enters the twilight zone below this
    threshold, where homology inference becomes statistically unreliable.
  \item Protein language models trained on large sequence corpora develop
    emergent representations that encode structural and functional signal;
    nearest-neighbour transfer in this embedding space recovers functional
    information that alignment cannot, providing the biological justification
    for PROTEA's core approach.
  \item Taxonomic classification provides additional context for annotation
    confidence and is used as a secondary scoring factor in PROTEA.
\end{itemize}

With this background in place, \cref{chap:related} reviews the landscape of
